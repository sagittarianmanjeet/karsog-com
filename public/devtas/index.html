Land of devtas

Devtas of Karsog

The main devtas and temples of the valley, the stories people tell about them, and their fairs.

Karsog is called a land of devtas: every area has its own devta, who travels in a rath or palki to fairs, visits other devtas and is consulted through the gur. Here are the main devtas and temples of the valley, what people believe about them and when their fairs are. Stories are given as local people tell them.

Shri Mool Mahunag Temple

श्री मूल माहूँनाग मंदिर
📍 Bakhari Kothi, near Karsog, Mandi district, Himachal Pradesh

Shri Mool Mahunag is the principal Naga deity of the Karsog valley, worshipped at Bakhari Kothi about 25 km from Karsog town. Locals identify him with Karna, the great warrior and giver of charity (Danveer Karna) from the Mahabharata. The temple is one of the most prominent deity seats in the wider Suket-Mandi region and hosts a large annual district-level fair as well as an old, close ritual relationship with the former royal family of Suket.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe Mahunag is a divine form (avatar) of Karna, the Mahabharata's great warrior known for his charity and fatal vow of friendship to Duryodhana.
  • Tradition holds that after the war Arjuna, remorseful over killing Karna by deceit, had his body cremated near Tattapani on the Sutlej with the help of naga friends; a naga is said to have emerged from that pyre and settled here, worshipped as Mahunag.
  • Local legend says Mahunag originated at Shendal in the former Suket state and protected the Suket king from captivity (accounts vary between Mughal and British) by his shakti; grateful, the king built a close ritual bond with the deity, later reflected in the Suket Devta Mela tradition.
  • Devotees believe Mahunag's blessing wards off ailments, enemies and misfortune, and that wronging his devotees brings karmic consequences.

Fairs

  • District-level Shri Mool Mahunag Mela — Usually starts around Jyeshtha Sankranti, roughly 14-15 May, and runs about 5 days
  • Suket Devta Mela, Sundernagar (Mahunag's visit) — Departs Bakhari Kothi for this state-level fair in March, walking roughly 120 km; return timing varies by year
  • Rath Yatra (chariot procession) with jatar hosting days — Booking announced around Ashwin Sankranti (mid-Sept); the yatra itself starts late October/November

Customs

  • Devotee families reserve a 'jatar' day at Bakhari Kothi to host the deity's rath during its touring yatra.
  • Gold is avoided as an offering since Mahunag's murti (idol) is silver.
  • On the last Monday of Shravan, devotees hold puja, bhajan-kirtan and small bhandaras at the temple.

Shri Mamleshwar Mahadev Temple

श्री ममलेश्वर महादेव मंदिर
📍 Mamel village, Karsog, Mandi district, Himachal Pradesh

Mamleshwar Mahadev is an ancient wood-and-stone Shiva temple in Mamel village near Karsog, regarded locally as one of the valley's oldest shrines. Tradition links it to the Pandavas' exile, and it preserves relics said to date from that period: an ever-burning sacred fire, a giant fossilised wheat grain, and a wooden drum attributed to Bhima. It also presides as senior deity over Karsog's main annual fair, the Nalwar Mela.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe the temple dates back roughly 5,000 years, spanning the Satya, Treta and Dwapara (Mahabharata) ages.
  • Tradition holds the Pandavas stayed in Mamel during their exile (agyatvas) and Bhima killed a man-eating demon terrorising the village, an event some connect to the origin of the name 'Karsog'.
  • The ever-burning sacred fire (akhand dhuni) is said to have been lit by Bhima to mark his victory over the demon, burning continuously since.
  • A large fossil-like wheat grain (roughly 150-250 grams by various accounts) is believed to be one grown by the Pandavas here, at a spot now marked by the Annapurna Mata (Kharasna/Tara Devi) temple.

Fairs

  • Nalwar Mela, Karsog (district-level fair) — Usually early-to-mid April, but depends yearly on official/High Court permission for the ground; sometimes reduced to a smaller trade fair
  • Lahaul Mela (Shiv-Parvati wedding celebration) — Fixed date, 18 April each year
  • Shravan (monsoon month) Monday observance and Kanwar Yatra — Every Monday in Shravan (roughly July-August); a Kanwar Yatra to Haridwar sets off around the same time

Customs

  • Devotees offer milk and water (doodh-jal) to the Shiva lingam on the last Shravan Monday.
  • The temple keeps an akhand dhuni (perpetually burning fire) regarded with deep reverence.
  • The Bhima drum and wheat grain are kept on display for darshan by pilgrims.

Maa Kamaksha Temple, Kao

माँ कामाक्षा मंदिर, काओ
📍 Kao village, about 7-8 km from Karsog town, Mandi district, Himachal Pradesh

Maa Kamaksha temple in Kao village is one of the main shakti (goddess) shrines of the Karsog valley, dedicated to Durga in her Kamaksha form. It is built in the traditional wood-and-stone pagoda style typical of the valley and is closely linked to legends of Parashurama and to the old Suket princely state, whose royal family is said to have worshipped Kamaksha as their family deity. Local posts sometimes describe it as one of only a few Kamaksha/Kamakhya temples in the region, alongside the well-known one in Guwahati, Assam (this is a local claim, not something the posts prove).

Beliefs & stories

  • According to one local tradition, Parashurama did penance at this spot after killing his mother Renuka, and a pleased Maa Kamaksha appeared to him; he then installed her image and founded the temple here.
  • Another local tradition says the goddess's ashtadhatu (eight-metal) idol was brought from a place called 'Kaoru', which is why the village is named Kao.
  • Locals believe the goddess slew the demon Mahishasura at this site, so she is worshipped here as Mahishasuramardini.
  • The temple is said to have served as the rajdevi (royal deity) of the former Suket princely state, and is also described locally as a kuldevi (family deity) shared by the Suket, Bushahr and Kullu ruling families.

Fairs

  • Ashtami Mela, Kao — Held during Sharadiya (autumn) Navratri, centred on the midnight of Ashtami
  • Barshail Mela, Lalag — 2-3 June, fixed
  • Kunho (Kunnu) Mela — 7-9 August, fixed, three days
  • Lahaul Mela — 18 April, fixed; symbolises the wedding of Shiva and Parvati
  • Suket Devta Mela, Sundernagar (Kamaksha's occasional attendance) — Same dates as the wider Suket Devta Mela (see [[suket_devta_mela]]); Kamaksha does not attend every year

Customs

  • A bed is made fresh every morning in a small room beside the main idol, in the belief that the goddess rests there at night.
  • The temple keeps a very large old dhol (drum), which local tradition associates with Bhima of the Mahabharata.
  • On Ashtami night during Navratri, the goddess is believed to give darshan through a local devotee who becomes possessed.

Shikari Devi Temple

शिकारी देवी मंदिर (शिकारी माता)
📍 Shikari peak, near Janjehli, Karsog area, Mandi district, Himachal Pradesh

Shikari Devi temple sits at roughly 3,300-3,360 metres (about 11,000 feet) on the highest peak in Mandi district, making it one of the highest temples in the region and earning it the nickname 'Crown of Mandi'. Its most famous feature is that the shrine has no roof, and locals believe that despite heavy snowfall the idol itself never gathers snow. Tradition connects the site to the Pandavas' exile and to the sage Markandeya's penance.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe the temple was established by the Pandavas during their period of exile (agyatvas), when they are said to have worshipped the goddess here in the form of a deer that appeared before them.
  • Tradition holds that sage Markandeya performed years of tapasya (penance) at this spot, and that Goddess Durga, pleased by his devotion, manifested here in her shakti form.
  • Locals believe that despite heavy snow all around the temple, snow never settles on the main idol or, in some accounts, within the temple courtyard itself.
  • According to local tradition, many attempts over the years to build a roof over the shrine have failed, and this is regarded as the goddess's own wish or a divine mystery.

Fairs

  • Navratri gathering at Shikari Devi — During Navratri (spring and autumn, per the Hindu lunar calendar)

Customs

  • Devotees make the difficult trek or drive-plus-climb (including around 500 steps at the end of the road journey) to reach the temple, considered part of the pilgrimage's significance.
  • A regular bus service connects Sundernagar to Shikari Devi via Chailchowk, Bagsyad, Thunag and Janjehli, used by pilgrims and tourists especially in season.
  • The temple and surrounding area falls within a designated wildlife sanctuary where hunting is prohibited, a detail some posts note in connection with the 'hunter's goddess' legend.

Kamrunag Temple & Lake

कमरूनाग मंदिर एवं झील
📍 Kamraah village area, high in the forested hills on the Mandi/Karsog side (Janjehli valley), Mandi district, Himachal Pradesh, at about 3,334 m (10,938 ft)

Kamrunag (also called Bada Dev, 'the great god') is worshipped across the wider Mandi district, including the Karsog valley, as the god of rain, and no rain-dependent activity or major mela in the district is traditionally begun without his blessing. His temple sits beside a small sacred lake deep in deodar forest, where devotees immerse gold, silver and coin offerings as a mark of faith. He is closely linked to several Karsog-area fairs and deities such as Bala Tikka ji and Shikari Mata.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals identify Kamrunag with Veer Barbarik (also called Khatu Shyam ji/Babrubhan ji) of the Mahabharata, said to have been one of the most powerful warriors on earth and eager to join the great war.
  • According to tradition, no rain or snow falls in the region without Kamrunag's will, so devotees and even district officials have historically sought his blessing in times of drought.
  • Mandi's own International Shivratri Mela is said locally not to begin until Kamrunag has given his consent, and the deity is ceremonially received by Mandi town before the Shivratri mela opens.

Fairs

  • Sarahanuli Mela — Held at the main Kamrunag temple; a fixed annual fair (mid-June in local usage, distinct from the 14 June date given in an older general description)
  • Maharan (Gohar) Mela — Held twice a year; exact dates vary and are announced each time
  • Rohanda Mela — 20 Ashadh (Hindu calendar), around early July
  • Suket Devta Mela, Sundernagar (Kamrunag's attendance) — Same dates as the wider mela (see [[suket_devta_mela]])

Customs

  • Devotees immerse gold, silver ornaments, coins and cash into the small lake beside the temple as offerings to the deity, a practice followed for generations.
  • The temple priest acts as a medium (gur) through whom the deity is believed to speak and act.
  • Devotees and organisers have repeatedly asked, in posts, that visitors keep the temple's surroundings quiet and not disturb it with loud bhajans, dholak or DJ music, out of respect for the deity's preference for a calm environment.

Gadhpati Shri Nag Chawasi Siddh

गढ़पति श्री नाग च्वासी सिद्ध
📍 Kothi Mahog, Chawasi area, Karsog, Mandi district

Nag Chawasi Siddh is the presiding deity ('gadhpati') of the Chawasi area of Karsog valley, seated at Mahog, ruling over roughly 16 panchayats including six other devi-devta temples. 2026 was unusually eventful, with several first-ever or century-plus-gap appearances at fairs outside his own area.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe that a king of the former Suket princely state once gifted Nag Chawasi Siddh a large cauldron (kadhai), still kept in the temple and used to hold the deity's ceremonial tongs.

Fairs

  • Nalwar Mela, Karsog (first-ever attendance) — District-level fair usually 5-11 April
  • Suket Devta Mela, Sundernagar — State-level fair, usually mid-to-late March
  • Rowa Mela, Himrigarh (historic meeting) — Late September
  • Sareulsar yatra (to meet mother Budhi Nagin) — Around Ashadh Sankranti

Customs

  • An annual 'Green Chawasi Siddh' campaign has women's groups (mahila mandals) plant trees on the deity's Prakatya Diwas (manifestation day).
  • The Mahog temple's foundation was laid in 2017, finished in 2025; Pran Pratishtha Maha Puj held 28 May-5 June 2025 with visiting deities from Suket, Magru, Mangarh, Raghupurgarh and Janjagarh.

Chindi Mata Temple

चिंडी माता मंदिर
📍 Chindi village, about 13-14 km from Karsog town on the Karsog-Shimla road, Mandi district, Himachal Pradesh, at roughly 1,400-1,825 m altitude

Chindi Mata temple, in Chindi village of the Karsog valley, is dedicated to Durga in her fierce Chandi (Chandika) form and is best known for the legend that its layout was mapped out by a colony of ants. The temple has an ancient eight-armed (ashtabhuji) stone idol and is built with fine traditional Himachali wood carving. It is one of the valley's main shakti shrines, drawing pilgrims from as far as Delhi, Kolkata and Mumbai, especially for its August fair.

Beliefs & stories

  • According to local legend, the goddess appeared to a priest in a dream as a young unmarried girl (kanya) and showed him, through a trail marked out by a colony of ants, the exact plan for the temple, and even for its adjoining pond and storeroom.
  • Locals say the temple's name 'Chindi' comes from 'chinti' (ant), because the site's layout was traced by the ants rather than by any human hand.
  • Chindi Mata is regarded locally as a kumari (unmarried) goddess who dedicated her life entirely to protecting dharma and her devotees rather than marrying.
  • One version of her journey story says she first set out from a place called Jufar, was welcomed as a sister by Dev Mahasu at the village of Shiva, then was so loved by the people of Bajo that she called that village her maternal home (maika), and finally settled permanently at Chindi after being captivated by its beauty.

Fairs

  • Chindi Mata Mela (Chakranth) — A fixed three-day fair, 2-4 August every year, held at the Chakranth mela ground near the temple

Customs

  • The temple keeps a bhandar (storeroom) where the goddess's costly garments and ornaments are stored, its layout also credited to the ants in legend.
  • During Navratri, the temple holds special puja, havan and kirtan along with a large community bhandara (free community meal).
  • The three-day annual fair begins and ends with a formal chariot procession between the temple and the mela ground rather than the goddess simply remaining at the temple.

Shri Nag Dhamuni

श्री नाग धमूनी
📍 Seri Bungalow (Seri Banglo), Karsog

Nag Dhamuni is described locally as 'king of four forts', with a domain covering more than half of the Karsog valley. His main temple is at Seri Bungalow, and he is closely tied to the Kunho village fairground and to the Ashadh/Thirshu fair season each June.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe Nag Dhamuni originally belonged to Dev Chhamahu (Chamahu) of Kullu district, and that he left Dhaman (in Kullu) upset, accompanied by an old woman, travelling toward the Suket (present Karsog) side before finally settling at the Dhamun-Seri Banglo area, which became his karambhoomi (adopted homeland).
  • Locals tell of a hill-lowering contest between Shri Mool Mahunag and Nag Dhamuni: angered by Dhamuni's trickery, Mahunag used his mace (gurj) to raise the hill back up, and since then the two deities have never met in person.

Fairs

  • Mela Seri Bhanju (Nag Panchami pragatotsav) — Starts on Rishi Panchami, a 3-day block-level fair at Seri Bungalow
  • Ashadh Mela / Thirshu Mela, Jachh-Chauridhar — Starts on Ashadh Sankranti, roughly 16-18 June
  • Kunho (Kunnu) Mela — 3-day fair, usually 7-9 August

Customs

  • Historically, Nag Dhamuni was among the deities who used to attend the Nalwar Mela in Karsog, alongside Dev Dawahadi and Nag Belu.

Mahamaya Devi Temple, Pangna

महामाया देवी मंदिर, पांगणा
📍 Pangna, Karsog subdivision, Mandi district

Mahamaya Devi's temple at Pangna is one of the oldest wooden temples in the Karsog area, said to have been built roughly 820 years ago by a Suket king. It stands in the traditional 'kashtkuni' (timber-bonded stone) style, valued locally for its earthquake resistance. The nearby Gihn Nag temple at Sarahi is also part of the wider Pangna deity landscape.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe the temple was founded after a king's daughter was buried alive for six months to prove her purity, and emerged unharmed; the temple was built to mark this event.
  • Locals say Sarahi village, near the Gihn Nag temple, was once the capital of a historic princely area referred to as 'Kushraj'.

Fairs

  • Katal Mela (Shri Gihn Nag), Pangna — Early June, around 8-11 June

Customs

  • The Mahamaya temple's wooden kashtkuni construction (stacked stone-and-timber courses pinned with large wooden pegs) is locally described as earthquake-resistant.
  • A full Devi Bhagwat Katha at the Mahamaya temple is a rare event, last held roughly 20-25 years before a September 2026 report that preparations for a new one were underway.
  • The Gihn Nag temple at Sarahi, built in the traditional 'kothi' style with carved doors and window screens, had a pedhi-aur-chhatar (chariot-canopy) pratishtha ceremony in 2025.

Shri Dev Mahasu

श्री देव महासु
📍 Bakhras village, Karsog area, Mandi district

Dev Mahasu of Bakhras is regarded locally as an avatar of Shiva and a god of justice, distinct from (though sharing a name and origin story with) the well-known four-brother Mahasu tradition of Hanol in Uttarakhand. He attends the annual Suket Devta Mela at Sundernagar from his seat at Bakhras Kothi.

Beliefs & stories

  • Locals believe Dev Mahasu is an avatar of Lord Shiva who appeared to establish dharma, destroy injustice and protect devotees, and that he delivers swift and true justice, which is why people bring disputes to his court.
  • Locals believe Mahasu takes four brother forms: Bashik Mahasu, Pabasi Mahasu, Chalda Mahasu and Sangla Mahasu, worshipped with varying local traditions across Himachal.
  • Locals believe that when Chindi Mata journeyed from Jufar, she first arrived and stayed at 'Shiva' (Shiva village), the seat of Dev Mahasu, who welcomed her and accepted her as his sister before she continued on to Bajo and eventually Chindi.

Fairs

  • Suket Devta Mela, Sundernagar (Dev Mahasu's visit) — Deity departs from Bakhras Kothi in March

Customs

  • A newly built palanquin (palki) for Dev Mahasu, crafted by a local artisan, was presented at Bisoi village, Khat Bahlad, in 2026.

Laxmi Narayan Temple

लक्ष्मी नारायण मंदिर
📍 Purana Bazaar, Karsog

The Laxmi Narayan temple in Karsog's Purana Bazaar is a working neighbourhood temple that hosts a daily evening aarti and also serves as an overnight halting point for visiting deities passing through Karsog town during major fairs.

Fairs

  • Janmashtami and Nand Utsav — August-September, on the Hindu calendar dates for Janmashtami and the following Nand Utsav

Customs

  • A daily evening aarti (sandhyakaleen aarti) is held at the temple, including for the Narsingh Bhagwan idol housed within the complex.
  • During the 2026 Nalwar Mela, the visiting deity Gadhpati Shri Nag Chawasi Siddh halted overnight at this temple after taking his evening meal at the Mamleshwar Mahadev temple, Mamel.

Shri Narsingh Dev

श्री नरसिंह देव
📍 Purana Bazaar, Karsog (idol within the Laxmi Narayan temple complex)

Narsingh Bhagwan is worshipped as part of the Laxmi Narayan temple complex in Karsog's Purana Bazaar, with a daily evening aarti. The deity also has an annual jayanti (birth anniversary) procession in which Karsog's Mool Mahunag participates.

Fairs

  • Shri Narsingh Dev Jayanti procession — Around mid-to-late May

Customs

  • A daily evening aarti for Narsingh Bhagwan is held at the Laxmi Narayan temple, Purana Bazaar, and is sometimes shared live.

Shiv Gufa (Shiva Cave), Saraur

शिव गुफा, सरौर
📍 Saraur, near Tattapani, Karsog subdivision

The Shiv Gufa at Saraur, near Tattapani, is an ancient natural cave shrine that was partly submerged by the Kol Dam reservoir on the Sutlej, making it hard to reach. It draws worshippers especially around Mahashivratri.

Fairs

  • Mahashivratri jagran / Shiv katha at Tattapani — Around Mahashivratri (Feb-March, Phalgun Krishna Chaturdashi)

Customs

  • The cave was submerged by the Kol Dam hydroelectric project on the Sutlej; a local visitor reported reaching it again in December 2025 after roughly 10 years, and documented its condition at the time.

Dev Dawahad (Dwahadi)

देव दवाहड़ी
📍 Karsog valley (deity tours the wider Karsog area)

Dev Dawahadi is described as the presiding deity ('aaradhya') of Karsog valley, known for periodically touring the region and staying as a guest in local homes. He shares an unusual chariot arrangement with Mamleshwar Mahadev.

Customs

  • Dev Dawahadi periodically undertakes a village-to-village tour of Karsog valley, staying as a guest in homes across the area as a form of blessing.
  • A devotee describes a rath (chariot) in which Dev Dawahadi's idol rides seated behind Mamleshwar Mahadev's, a pairing said to be possibly unique in Himachal Pradesh; a related chariot also carries 'Dev Ledhi ji'.
  • Historically, Dev Dawahadi (along with Nag Belu and Nag Dhamuni) was among the deities who used to attend the Nalwar Mela in Karsog.

Shri Kajauni Nag

श्री कजौणी नाग
📍 Mamel, Karsog

Nag Kajauni is seated at Mamel, close to the Mamleshwar Mahadev temple, and regularly appears alongside Mamleshwar and other Karsog deities at joint fairs and processions.

Fairs

  • Nalwar Mela, Karsog — District-level fair, usually 5-11 April

Customs

  • Nag Kajauni's chariot is regularly seen alongside Maa Kamaksha and Nag Pundri at the Kao Lahaul Mela.
  • Historically noted alongside Mamleshwar Mahadev, Nag Dhamuni and Nag Sayanj Bagda at a 'devon ka alaukik milan' (divine gathering) at Bhandarnu.

Nag Pundri

नाग पुंडरी
📍 Kao, near Karsog (shares the Maa Kamaksha temple precinct)

Nag Pundri travels closely alongside Maa Kamaksha of Kao on nearly all her major journeys, and is also one of the three deities honoured at the annual Kunho Mela along with Nag Dhamuni.

Fairs

  • Kunho (Kunnu) Mela — 3-day fair, usually 7-9 August
  • Barshail Mela, Lalag — Fixed dates, 2-3 June

Customs

  • Nag Pundri also attended the state-level Suket Devta Mela at Sundernagar alongside Maa Kamaksha in 2025.

Nag Tundli

श्री नाग तुंदली
📍 Tundal village, Nanj, Karsog

Nag Tundli is the presiding deity of Tundal village in Nanj, honoured each November with a two-day village fair that had lapsed for two decades before being revived in 2022.

Fairs

  • Nag Tundli Mela, Nanj — Fixed dates, 15-16 November

Customs

  • The fair's opening evening centres on the deity's own dev nritya (ritual dance), performed to devotional drumbeats before the wider cultural programme.

Shri Nag Sunani & Shri Kot Nag

श्री नाग सुनानी व श्री कोट नाग
📍 Banglaadhar, Churag panchayat, Karsog

Nag Sunani and Dev Thala are honoured together each year at a fair in Banglaadhar, Churag, marking the ceremonial meeting (milan) of the two deities.

Fairs

  • Nag Sunani & Dev Thala Mela, Banglaadhar (Churag) — Set by the Hindu calendar, on 29 Shravan

Pali Nag

पाली नाग
📍 Kali village, Karsog

Pali Nag is a village deity at Kali, Karsog, whose story is kept alive locally through folk performance rather than large public fairs in the material seen so far.

Beliefs & stories

  • Local folk artists perform a traditional piece about 'Pali Nag Maharaj' and the sacred confluence (sangam) of Imla and Bimla, suggesting a local legend around these paired sacred sites/streams, though the full story was not described in the post itself.

Customs

  • A senior folk artist from Panjrat village is noted for performing this Pali Nag Maharaj/Imla-Bimla piece as part of a decades-long repertoire of local Karsog folk theatre and Karyala performances.

Edhneshwar Mahadev

एधनेश्वर महादेव
📍 Syanj Bagra, Karsog

Edhneshwar Mahadev's temple at Syanj Bagra hosts the annual Thirshu Mela, a multi-day fair known for its cultural evenings with well-known regional folk singers.

Fairs

  • Thirshu Mela, Edhneshwar Mahadev — Held around late April/springtime as part of the wider Thirshu fair season
